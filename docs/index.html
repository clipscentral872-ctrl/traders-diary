      Where your trades live

      Nowhere but this device. The files you drop are read in the browser, and the record is kept in this browser's own storage. Nothing is uploaded, there is no account, and no one else can see it, including whoever made this. That also means clearing your browser data clears the diary, so keep a backup.

      One exception, and it is worth knowing about. This copy ships with a starting record already in it, so a fresh install opens with something to look at instead of an empty screen. That record is part of the published site, which is public, so anyone with the link can read those trades. Press Erase everything and it is gone for good on this device: it is never put back. Everything you import after that is yours alone.

      Lock it with a passcode

      A passcode does not just hide the diary behind a screen, it encrypts the record. What is left in this browser afterwards is a blob that means nothing without the passcode, so someone holding your unlocked phone gets nothing out of it.

      The cost of that is real and there is no way around it: there is no reset. No account, no recovery email, nobody with a spare key. Forget the passcode and the record is gone. Save a backup file first, and keep it somewhere you trust, because the backup stays unencrypted on purpose so that it is a way back in rather than a second thing to lose.

      Set a passcode

      Longer beats clever. A short phrase you will not forget is stronger than a word with symbols in it.

          The automated system

          A version of the four-step method runs on its own against live bars and reports here. It is not connected to your account and never will be from this page. It exists to answer one question honestly: does the method make money when a human is not choosing which setups to take.

            Two books, same bars

            The same setups are traded twice at once, at different reward-to-risk shapes. A book that takes a small target wins most of its trades by design, which looks like an edge and is not one. Running both side by side on identical bars is the only way to tell the shape apart from the edge.

            Every setup it was offered

            The system logs each setup it sees, whether or not it takes it. That log is what settled the question of why its early record was almost all longs.

            The method, in four steps

            This is the sequence the system looks for and the one your own trades are read against. Each step has to happen in order. A setup that skips one is not a setup.

            1. Liquidity is taken

              Price runs past an obvious high or low, where stops are resting, and then fails to continue. The run is the point: it is what fills the orders the move needs. Without a sweep there is nothing to reverse.

            2. Structure breaks on the five minute

              After the sweep, price breaks the other way through the last swing that mattered. This is the first evidence the sweep was a turn rather than a pause.

            3. Something backs it up

              A gap left by the break, or price returning to the middle of the move. One reason is not enough on its own; this is the step that separates a break worth trading from noise.

            4. Enter on the one minute

              Only now, and on the smaller timeframe, so the stop can sit behind the structure rather than out in the noise. The entry is the last decision, not the first.

            Where the money is actually lost

            Read from your own record rather than from a book. These are the things this diary has caught in your trading, in the order they cost you the most.

              The revisit number on the chart

              Next to the previous day's high and low, while they are still untapped, the chart shows how often a level like that one actually got tapped before the day closed. It is measured from the published bars, in your browser, not asserted.

              Every bar in the history where a previous-day level was still untapped is one observation, filed by two things: how far price was from it as a share of the average daily range, and how much of the session had gone. At the close each observation is settled as tapped or not. The number you see is simply how often that bucket got tapped.

              Three things keep it honest. A bucket with few observations is pulled toward the overall base rate rather than believed, because three out of three is not a certainty and printing 100% would be a lie with a number on it. A thin bucket is marked with a ? so you can see the sample is small. And nothing looks ahead: the range used to measure distance comes from the days before, never from the whole history.

              What it is not. It does not know about news, it does not know what you are looking at, and it is built from about sixty days. A high number is not a trade and a low one is not a reason to fade something. It is background: whether you are in a market that usually goes back for these, or one that usually does not.

              Two rules this will not bend

              • R is the risk you took at entry Never the trailed stop. Measuring against the stop you trailed to divides a small loss by a tiny remaining risk. On real trades that produced -22R on one of them, and turned a day that made money into an expectancy of -3.8R. If it looks impossible, it is.
              • No stop on record means no R Not zero, and not R measured against whatever stop happens to be known. A trade with no stop has no risk you decided in advance, so it has no R and no position size that could have been worked out beforehand. The diary says so rather than filling the gap in.
